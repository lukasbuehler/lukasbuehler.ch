Recently, I thought a lot about the future of software development and my place in it.
Coding agents powered by the most recent Large Language Models (LLMs) are an integral part of writing software now.
But this is a very recent development, and I was there for the whole ride.
I tried ChatGPT the week it dropped. I watched LLMs stumble over basic syntax in simple functions, then, some months later, get the right idea but mess up the details and send me on a debugging spree.
I watched them go from gimmick, to helpful, to making me more efficient at solving coding problems, to being better at almost everything in software development.

To me, a coding agent now is like a pretty experienced, over-eager junior developer. Give it a task and it will just go and do what it thinks is right, which may not be how you wanted it, but it did something. (Yay I guess.) That can be super cool.
But most of the time I want to know what's going on in the codebase, how things are structured and architected, because that's my job and I also enjoy the software design part.

Lately, I've been as productive as ever. I had fun shipping cool side projects at a pace like never before, but I felt like I was missing something.
And then it clicked: I miss the puzzles.

I don't mind not having to write the syntax. But I am dearly missing the problem-solving aspect of coding. I mean the small puzzles inside the bigger puzzle that is the project. Even if it meant looking up an API definition or digging through framework documentation.
Typing the magic words and making it do what you wanted was always so rewarding to me. And that is gone now.
And it will never be the same.

I still think about the high-level problems, more than ever, but it's not as rewarding as it used to be.

Of course, you can still do it if you really want to, but it's like walking somewhere when you could also teleport there, for cheap.
It's less efficient and slows down your progress. Just optional. You could also just go to [LeetCode](https://leetcode.com/).

There used to be no better or more cost-effective way than me getting my fingers dirty in code. And I loved it.
Almost like I had an excuse to solve these puzzles and then call it 'working'.
There were times it was monotonous perhaps, but never boring when it was necessary.
Now it almost never is necessary anymore, making the puzzle solving unnecessary.

And don't get me wrong, I don't want to go back. I love what coding agents allow me to build, and I don't  want to trade that productivity away.

But I do miss the little puzzles. The satisfaction of understanding a problem, figuring out a solution, and watching all those small pieces come together into something bigger.

Maybe that's simply what progress looks like. We have abstract away the problems we've learned to solve and move on to bigger ones.

But where does the interesting work move when implementation becomes cheap?
I don't have a complete answer yet. I suspect I'll spend quite some time thinking about that.
And maybe that's the next interesting problem to solve for me.
